How to Read a Food Label: A 5-Step Guide

What the label must show under Turkish food law, the order in which to read it, and the 2024 changes that businesses must apply by 31 December 2026.

← All articles

English · Türkçe

Bardino · 15 October 2026

You can read a food label in five steps: the real name of the product, the ingredients list, the allergens printed in emphasis, the nutrition table per 100 g or 100 ml, and finally the date and storage instructions. The order is not arbitrary. Most of the information that Turkey's food labelling regulation makes mandatory fits under these five headings1. The pictures and slogans on the front of the pack are not mandatory information; the regulation requires, among other things, that they do not mislead you.

Which rules this article uses. This guide was first written for shoppers in Turkey and follows the Turkish Food Codex regulation on food labelling. That regulation says it was prepared with the EU's Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 in mind, as part of aligning with EU law1. Where we compare with the EU, we checked the EU text6, 7; where we say nothing, do not assume the rule is the same elsewhere.

What the Label Must Show

Under Article 9 of the Turkish regulation, the label of a prepacked food must show: the name of the food, the list of ingredients, substances or products that cause allergies or intolerances, the quantity of certain ingredients where required, the net quantity, the "best before" or "use by" date, any special storage or use conditions, the name and address of the responsible business, the business registration number, the country of origin, instructions for use where needed, the alcohol strength for drinks with more than 1.2% alcohol by volume, and the nutrition declaration1. Article 9 of the EU regulation has the same list, except that it has no business registration number and requires the country of origin only in the cases set out in its Article 266.

This information must be printed in letters with an x-height of at least 1.2 mm; on packs whose largest surface is smaller than 80 cm², the minimum is 0.9 mm1. The EU sets the same sizes6. The text must not be hidden or covered and must contrast clearly with the background. A label too small or too faint to read is not only a problem with your eyesight; it is a problem under the regulation.

Step 1: Find the Product's Real Name

A brand name is not the name of the food. The regulation says so plainly: no trade mark or fancy name that does not describe the contents can replace the name of the food1. The EU rule is the same (Article 17(4))6. A pack whose pictures suggest "chocolate" may be named "cocoa-coated biscuit"; that is the line to look for.

A Turkish amendment of 6 April 2024 tightened this2:

  • The name of the food and the net quantity must appear in the principal field of vision (the side where the brand is printed largest). All words of the name must be in the same size, font and style. For comparison, the EU regulation requires the name and net quantity to be in the same field of vision (Article 13(5))6.
  • If a product uses only flavouring in place of a food ingredient such as fruit, a real photograph of that ingredient cannot be used; if a drawing is used, the word "flavoured" (aromalı) must appear wherever the drawing appears2, 3.
  • For products that could be confused with each other, wording such as "... taste", "... flavour" or "... delight" cannot be used with the name of another food whose characteristics the product does not have. The Ministry's guideline gives two examples: a cocoa-based coating cannot say "chocolate taste", and a margarine cannot say "butter taste"3.
  • For products with sweeteners, "with sweetener(s)" (Tatlandırıcılı or Tatlandırıcı(lar) içerir; with added sugar, "with sugar(s) and sweetener(s)") was already required next to the name under the 2017 regulation. It must now appear with the name wherever the name appears, in emphasised type1, 2.

The amendment came into force in 2024, and businesses have until 31 December 2026 to comply; after that date, foods that do not comply may not be on the market2. So in shops in Turkey today you may see labels printed under both the old and the new rules.

Step 2: Read the Ingredients List from the Top

Ingredients are listed in descending order of weight, as used in manufacture1, 6. The first ingredients are the ones the product contains most of. In a "hazelnut wafer", if hazelnuts appear near the end of the list, their share is small. In addition, an ingredient named in the product name, or emphasised on the label in words or pictures, must have its percentage given, apart from the exceptions in the regulation1. Look for the small percentage next to "hazelnuts".

Three details to watch in the list:

  • Group names. Sucrose may be listed as "sugar" and anhydrous dextrose as "dextrose". Refined vegetable oils may be grouped as "vegetable oils (palm, sunflower)"; partially hardened oils must carry the words "partially hydrogenated"1. When several ingredients of the same kind are listed separately, each one can drop further down the list. We explain how this works for sugar in our article on hidden sugar and salt.
  • Additives. The functional class comes first, then the name or E number: "preservative (potassium sorbate)" or "preservative (E202)"1, 6. For the list of classes and EFSA's assessments, see our E numbers list.
  • "Flavouring". If flavouring is used in place of fruit, it shows up here; if the pack has a drawing of the fruit, the 2024 rule also puts "flavoured" on the front.

Step 3: Check the Allergens in Emphasis

The 14 allergen groups in Annex 1 of the regulation (cereals containing gluten, crustaceans, eggs, fish, peanuts, soya, milk, tree nuts, celery, mustard, sesame, sulphur dioxide and sulphites above a set level, lupin, molluscs) are emphasised in the ingredients list so that they stand out from the other ingredients, for example in bold1. The EU has the same 14 groups and the same emphasis rule (Article 21 and Annex II)6.

"May contain (traces of) ..." is not mandatory. According to the Ministry's guideline, it may be used voluntarily, and only where a thorough risk assessment shows that cross-contamination cannot be avoided. General statements such as "all products may contain all allergens" do not count as allergen information3. More on children's allergies is in our guide to food allergy labels for children; for gluten, see choosing gluten-free products.

Step 4: Convert the Nutrition Table to Your Portion

The mandatory nutrition declaration has seven lines: energy, fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein and salt1. Values must be given per 100 g or 100 ml; a per-portion value is an optional extra, and when it is given the portion size must be stated close to the table1. The EU rules are the same (Articles 30, 32 and 33)6.

LineDefinition in the regulationWhen you read it
CarbohydrateAny carbohydrate metabolised by humans, including polyolsCovers starch, sugars and sugar alcohols together
SugarsAll mono- and disaccharides, excluding polyolsDoes not separate added sugar from sugar that comes from fruit or milk
SaltSodium × 2.5If the label gives sodium, multiply by 2.5
%RIReference intake of an average adult (8,400 kJ / 2,000 kcal)Optional; based on an adult reference, so it does not show what a child needs

Converting to a portion is one multiplication: (value per 100 g × grams you eat) ÷ 100. If a biscuit has 28 g of sugar per 100 g and you eat 40 g, you get 11.2 g of sugar. The regulation's reference intake for total sugars is 90 g, so the label may show this as "12%"1; the EU uses the same reference values6. The World Health Organization's upper limit for free sugars is about 50 g in a 2,000 kcal diet5. The two numbers do not measure the same thing: 90 g is a reference for total sugars, including those in milk and fruit, while 50 g is an upper limit for added sugars plus the sugars in honey, syrups and fruit juice.

In Turkey, red, amber and green colours may not be used when showing %RI1. If you are used to traffic-light labels in other countries, do not look for colours on a Turkish nutrition table.

"Sugar-free", "no added sugar", "low sugar" on the front

Each of these has a legal condition4. Sugar-free: no more than 0.5 g of sugars per 100 g or 100 ml. Low sugar: no more than 5 g per 100 g for solids, or 2.5 g per 100 ml for liquids. No added sugar: no mono- or disaccharides, and no other food used for its sweetening properties, may have been added; if the product contains sugars naturally, the label must also say "contains naturally occurring sugars". The EU sets the same three conditions in Regulation (EC) No 1924/20067. None of them means "carbohydrate-free" or "calorie-free".

Step 5: Read the Date and Storage Instructions

There are two kinds of date on a label, and they mean different things1:

  • "Use by": used on foods that spoil easily from a microbiological point of view. Under the regulation, a food past this date is considered unsafe1. The EU rule is the same (Article 24)6.
  • "Best before": shows how long the food keeps its specific qualities when stored properly; it is a quality indicator.

A "best before" date may be given as day and month for products that keep for less than 3 months, month and year for 3 to 18 months, and year only for longer. Some products need no "best before" date at all, such as fresh fruit and vegetables, vinegar, non-iodised salt, solid sugar and chewing gum1. The EU uses the same date formats (Annex X)6. If the label gives a time limit such as "once opened, keep refrigerated and eat within 3 days", count those days from the day you open the pack; the regulation requires storage conditions and the time limit after opening to be stated where needed1.

The front of the pack is a shop window

The regulation says voluntary information must not be misleading, ambiguous or confusing1, but the producer still decides what to put on the front. Decide on the basis of the ingredients list and nutrition table on the back, not on a single claim on the front.

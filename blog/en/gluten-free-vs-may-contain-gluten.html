Choosing Gluten-Free Products: "Gluten-Free" vs "May Contain Gluten"

The gluten statements allowed by law, their numerical limits, and how gluten-containing cereals appear in the ingredients list.

← All articles

English · Türkçe

Bardino · 15 October 2026

"Gluten-free" is a measured limit: under the Turkish Food Codex, the statement may only be used on food that contains no more than 20 mg of gluten per kilogram as sold1. The EU sets the same limit6. "May contain gluten" or "may contain traces of gluten" is not a measurement but a voluntary warning: it says that unintended cross-contamination during production cannot be avoided. Neither the Turkish regulation nor the Ministry's guideline sets a numerical limit for it1, 2. So the first tells you the most gluten the product can contain; the second does not.

The Gluten Statements in the Rules

Turkey's labelling regulation allows only the statements listed in its Annex 16 about the absence or reduced presence of gluten1. The Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry's labelling guideline sets out these statements and the conditions for the "may contain" warning in more detail2. The 2024 amendment (Article 6) also obliged food businesses to follow these guidelines; the deadline for complying with the new provisions introduced through the guideline is 31 December 20262, 3. In the EU, the same subject is covered by Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) No 828/20146.

The guideline also says that the logo registered by Turkey's Ministry of Health, or similar logos that do not mislead consumers, may be used on gluten-free foods. Additional statements (such as "suitable for people with coeliac disease") may not be more prominent than the main statement2. On infant formula and follow-on formula, no statement about the absence or reduced presence of gluten may be used at all1; the EU has the same ban6.

Can naturally gluten-free foods say "gluten-free"?

Yes. According to the guideline, these statements may be used on all food groups covered by the Turkish Food Codex, provided the 20 mg/kg condition is met2. So you may see "gluten-free" on a pack of rice or lentils too. The statement saying that a product was made especially for people with coeliac disease is different: "specifically formulated for ..." may only be used on products whose gluten-containing ingredient has been reduced in gluten or replaced with naturally gluten-free ingredients1. A bread made with naturally gluten-free flours instead of wheat flour, for example, can carry it.

No "may contain" warning does not mean no contamination

The warning is voluntary; what is mandatory is emphasising the gluten-containing cereals actually used as ingredients. And because no upper limit is set for products that do say "may contain", you cannot tell from the label how much gluten they contain. Talk to your dietitian about whether to eat these products.

How Gluten Appears in the Ingredients List

In Annex 1 of the Turkish regulation, the allergen group for gluten is "cereals containing gluten": wheat (including spelt and kamut), rye, barley, oats, their hybridised strains and products made from them1. These cereals are written in the ingredients list by name and emphasised so that they stand out from the other ingredients. Examples from the guideline: "wheat flour (gluten)", "kamut (wheat)", "barley"2. The EU group and the emphasis rule are the same (Annex II and Article 21 of Regulation 1169/2011)7.

Annex 1 leaves some ingredients out of the allergen declaration entirely: wheat-based glucose syrups including dextrose, wheat-based maltodextrins, barley-based glucose syrups, and cereals used to make alcoholic distillates. A footnote extends the exemption to products made from the wheat-based ones, as long as the process they have gone through is not likely to increase the level of allergenicity1. The EU annex has the same exemptions and footnote7. If you see "wheat-based glucose syrup" without emphasis, the label may be relying on this exemption; if you are unsure, ask the producer or your dietitian.

The NHS notes that processed foods in particular can contain gluten-based ingredients such as malt flavouring, and advises checking the label of every product you buy, every time4.

Loose Foods and Restaurants

Allergen information is not limited to prepacked products. According to the Turkish guideline, allergenic ingredients must also be declared for foods sold loose, foods packed at the counter at the customer's request, foods packed for direct sale on the premises, and foods served in restaurants, canteens, schools, hospitals and other mass caterers2. The information can be given on a menu, poster or board, and in addition by a QR code or digital screen2. In Turkey, the regulation also requires all mandatory information about a loose food to be given to anyone who asks for it1, so asking at a bakery or restaurant which items contain gluten-containing cereals is a request for information that you are entitled to. In the EU, allergen information for non-prepacked food is mandatory, and member states decide how it is provided (Article 44 of Regulation 1169/2011)7. Note that this information covers gluten used as an ingredient; ask separately about contamination from shared counters or ovens.

Why Oats Are a Separate Issue

Turkish law lists oats among the cereals containing gluten1. The NHS, on the other hand, says that oats do not contain gluten, but that many people with coeliac disease avoid them because they can be contaminated with gluten-containing cereals. It adds that there is some evidence that avenin, a protein in oats, can cause symptoms in a very small number of people even without contamination. The NHS says that people who want to add oats to their diet should talk to their care team first, check that the oats are pure, and avoid oats that are not labelled gluten-free4. The law is clear on this too: oats in a product sold as "gluten-free" or "very low gluten" must be specially produced to avoid contamination with wheat, rye and barley, and their gluten content may not exceed 20 mg/kg1. The EU rule is the same6.

Before the Shelf: Diagnosis and a Dietitian

Coeliac disease is treated by removing gluten from the diet for life, and the NHS says people who are newly diagnosed are referred to a dietitian4. Do not start a gluten-free diet on your own before diagnosis: the NHS advises continuing to eat gluten during testing so the results are accurate, and not switching to a gluten-free diet until a specialist has confirmed the diagnosis5. Contamination can also happen if gluten-free and gluten-containing foods are prepared together or served with the same utensils4.

Four Questions at the Shelf

  1. Does the pack say "gluten-free" or "very low gluten"? Which one?
  2. Does the ingredients list show wheat, rye, barley, oats or products made from them in emphasis?
  3. If the product contains oats, does it also carry a gluten-free statement?
  4. Is there a "may contain traces of gluten" warning? If so, what does your dietitian advise for this product?

Grocery Shopping with Diabetes: Carbohydrate and Sugar on the Label

Which line to read, how to work out a portion, and what "sugar-free" does and does not mean.

← All articles

English · Türkçe

Bardino · 15 October 2026

When you read a label with diabetes, the main line to look at is "carbohydrate", not "sugars". Under the Turkish Food Codex, the "sugars" line shows only mono- and disaccharides; starch and sugar alcohols (polyols) are counted within total carbohydrate1. The EU uses the same definitions7. In the words of the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), sugars and starches raise your blood sugar, but fibre does not2. The second rule: values are given per 100 g, but you eat a portion. How much carbohydrate you should eat in a day differs from person to person; that is for your diabetes team to set, not the label.

What the Lines in the Nutrition Table Tell You

The mandatory nutrition declaration covers energy, fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein and salt. Fibre, starch and polyols are optional extra lines1, 7. The definitions in the regulation for the lines that matter for diabetes:

LineIn the regulation1When you read it
CarbohydrateAny carbohydrate metabolised by humans, including polyolsCovers sugars, starch and polyols together. This is the main line.
SugarsAll monosaccharides and disaccharides in the food, excluding polyolsDoes not separate added sugar from the natural sugar in milk and fruit.
Starch (optional)Not defined; may be given as an optional lineIf it is not given, it may make up most of the gap between carbohydrate and sugars.
Polyols (optional)Alcohols with more than two hydroxyl groups (sugar alcohols)Included in carbohydrate; counted as 2.4 kcal per gram for energy, erythritol as 0.
Fibre (optional)Carbohydrate polymers that are neither digested nor absorbed in the small intestineNot digested, by definition; shown separately from the carbohydrate line.

In practice this means a cracker with a low "sugars" line can still be high in carbohydrate because of the starch in its flour. Labelling rules in other countries can differ, so check with your diabetes team before applying a carbohydrate-counting method from a foreign source to a Turkish label.

From 100 Grams to Your Portion

Nutrition values must be given per 100 g or 100 ml; a per-portion value is optional, and if it is given, the portion size must be stated close to the nutrition declaration1, 7. The calculation:

Carbohydrate you eat = (carbohydrate per 100 g × grams you eat) ÷ 100
Example: 40 g of a breakfast cereal with 72 g of carbohydrate per 100 g → 72 × 40 ÷ 100 = 28.8 g of carbohydrate. The carbohydrate in any milk or fruit you add is not included in this number.

Whose portion is the portion on the label?

When a Turkish label gives a value per portion, the portion sizes in Annex 12 of the regulation are used1. A few examples: biscuits 30 g, crackers 15 g, bread 50 g, breakfast cereal 30 g, muesli 40 g, dried fruit 30 g, fruit juice 250 ml, yoghurt 200 g. These values apply to people over the age of 41. They are standard units for calculation, not adjusted to your bowl or plate. Weighing a product you eat often, once, shows the difference between "1 portion" and what you actually eat.

Values marked "%RI" on the label are based on the daily reference intake of an average adult; for carbohydrate this is 260 g1, the same figure as in the EU7. This number is not your personal target. According to the CDC, many people with diabetes count carbohydrates to manage their blood sugar, and those who take mealtime insulin do it to match their insulin dose to what they eat. On how much carbohydrate to eat, the CDC says there is no one-size-fits-all answer: it depends on factors such as age, weight and activity level2. Set your target and your counting method with your doctor, diabetes nurse educator or dietitian.

What the Claims on the Front Say About Carbohydrate

Under Turkey's nutrition claims regulation, the conditions for sugar claims on the front of the pack are3:

  • Sugar-free: no more than 0.5 g of sugars per 100 g or 100 ml.
  • Low sugar: no more than 5 g of sugars per 100 g for solids, or 2.5 g per 100 ml for liquids.
  • No added sugar: no added mono- or disaccharides and no other food used for its sweetening properties; if the product contains sugar of its own, the label must say "contains naturally occurring sugars".

The EU sets the same three conditions in Regulation (EC) No 1924/20068. All three claims are only about sugars. A "sugar-free" biscuit can be high in carbohydrate because it contains flour; a "no added sugar" product made from fruit still contains the fruit's own sugar. After you see a claim on the front, look at the carbohydrate line on the back anyway.

A comparison with hypothetical numbers: biscuit A says "no added sugar" on the front and has 68 g of carbohydrate and 4 g of sugars per 100 g. Biscuit B makes no such claim and has 72 g of carbohydrate and 24 g of sugars per 100 g. In a 30 g portion you get 20.4 g of carbohydrate from A and 21.6 g from B. There is a sixfold difference in the sugars lines, but only 1.2 g in carbohydrate, because most of the carbohydrate in both biscuits comes from the starch in the flour.

Sweeteners and polyols

Turkish labels have had to show "with sweetener(s)" next to the name of the food since the 2017 regulation; the 2024 amendment requires it, in emphasised type, wherever the name appears, and businesses must comply by 31 December 20261, 4. In the EU, the statement must accompany the name of the food7. Products with more than 10% added polyols carry "excessive consumption may produce laxative effects"1, 7. WHO's 2023 guideline advising against non-sugar sweeteners for weight control does not apply to people with pre-existing diabetes, and it does not count sugar alcohols as non-sugar sweeteners5. Talk to your diabetes team about whether products with sweeteners suit you.

Finding Sugar Sources in the Ingredients

The ingredients list is in descending order of weight, and sugars can appear under different names: sucrose as "sugar", dextrose as "dextrose", glucose syrup as "glucose syrup"1. WHO's definition of free sugars also covers the sugars in honey, syrups, fruit juices and fruit juice concentrates6. If one or more of these is near the top of the list, much of the product's sugar probably comes from them. How sugar can be spread across the list is explained in our article on hidden sugar and salt.

A Five-Step Check at the Shelf

  1. Read the carbohydrate line per 100 g in the nutrition table.
  2. Weigh the amount you will eat or check the portion information on the pack, and convert the carbohydrate to that portion.
  3. Look at the sugars and fibre lines to see how much of the carbohydrate is sugar.
  4. Check whether sugar, syrup, honey or fruit juice concentrate is near the top of the ingredients.
  5. Weigh the front-of-pack claims ("sugar-free", "no added sugar", "with sweetener(s)") against this information.

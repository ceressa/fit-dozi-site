E Numbers List: What Does Each Food Additive Do?

The additive classes you see most often on labels, an example E number for each, and the latest EFSA conclusion on every example.

← All articles

English · Türkçe

Bardino · 15 October 2026

An E number is the code under which a food additive is approved in the European Union1. On its own it tells you neither that a substance is harmful nor that it is "natural". What an additive does is written on the label itself: in the ingredients list, additives are named first by their function (preservative, emulsifier, colour and so on) and then by their specific name or E number2, 22. The table below brings together the functional classes most often seen on supermarket labels, an example E number for each, and the most recent conclusion of the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) on that example.

Which rules this article uses. This guide was first written for readers in Turkey and cites the Turkish Food Codex. Turkey's additives regulation states that it was prepared in parallel with the EU's Regulation (EC) No 1333/20081, and its labelling regulation that it was prepared with Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 in mind2. Where we give the EU rule, we checked it in the EU text itself. Rules outside Turkey and the EU may differ.

What an E Number Does and Does Not Tell You

The Turkish additives regulation allows a substance onto the list of permitted additives only if it meets three conditions: on the scientific evidence available, it raises no safety concern at the proposed level of use; there is a reasonable technological need for it; and its use does not mislead the consumer1. The EU regulation sets the same three conditions in its Article 621. The level of use is set at the lowest amount that achieves the intended effect, taking into account the additive's acceptable daily intake (ADI) and the likely daily intake from all sources. For some additives no numerical maximum is set; the quantum satis rule then applies: use in line with good manufacturing practice, at a level no higher than needed for the purpose, and without misleading the consumer1, 21.

EFSA defines the ADI as an estimate of the amount of a substance that can be consumed every day over a lifetime without an appreciable health risk, usually given in milligrams per kilogram of body weight per day3. EFSA is re-evaluating the 315 additives that were authorised in the EU before 20 January 2009; its topic page says more than 70% of them have been re-evaluated4. The years in the table below are the publication years of the relevant EFSA opinions.

Not everything that looks like an additive is one

Under the Turkish regulation, sugars (mono-, di- and oligosaccharides), inulin, edible gelatin, milk protein, casein and caseinates, gluten, chewing gum bases, dextrins and some starches, such as physically or enzymatically treated starch, are not considered food additives1. Article 3 of the EU regulation has a matching list21.

How Additives Appear on the Label

In the ingredients list, an additive starts with the name of its functional class, followed by the specific name of the substance or its E number: "preservative (potassium sorbate)" and "preservative (E202)" are both correct. If an additive does more than one job, it is listed under its main function in that product. For modified starches the specific name or E number does not have to be given2. The EU rule is the same (Annex VII, Part C of Regulation 1169/2011)22. The Turkish regulation defines 26 functional classes; those you see most often on labels are below1.

Preservative Extends shelf life by delaying spoilage caused by micro-organisms E202 potassium sorbate 2019: group ADI of 11 mg/kg body weight per day, expressed as sorbic acid5
Preservative (meat products) Same class; used in processed meat products E250 sodium nitrite 2017: ADI of 0.07 mg nitrite ion/kg per day. Intake from additive use does not exceed the ADI in the general population, but slightly exceeds it in children with the highest intake. The panel also noted that epidemiological studies gave "some evidence" linking dietary nitrite to gastric cancer, and nitrite and nitrate from processed meat to colorectal cancer6
Antioxidant Delays deterioration caused by oxidation, such as fat rancidity and colour changes E321 BHT 2012: ADI of 0.25 mg/kg per day. Estimated to be exceeded by children with the highest intake (95th percentile) in some countries7
Colour Adds colour to food or restores colour lost in processing E120 carmine (cochineal) 2015: ADI of 2.5 mg carminic acid/kg per day. The panel noted that no threshold dose can be set for allergic reactions8
Emulsifier Keeps phases that do not mix, such as oil and water, evenly blended E322 lecithins, E471 mono- and diglycerides 2017: no numerical ADI needed for either; no safety concern at the reported uses9, 10
Thickener / gelling agent Adds body or forms a gel E415 xanthan gum, E440 pectin 2017: no numerical ADI needed for either. At high doses xanthan gum caused abdominal discomfort in some people; the panel judged this undesirable but not adverse11, 12
Acid / raising agent Acids increase acidity or give a sour taste; raising agents release gas and make dough rise E338 phosphoric acid, E450 diphosphates 2019: group ADI of 40 mg/kg per day, expressed as phosphorus. Intake from the whole diet, including naturally occurring phosphorus, was estimated to exceed it at average intake in infants, toddlers and children, and at the 95th percentile in adolescents too; intake from food supplements can also exceed it13
Flavour enhancer Strengthens the existing taste and smell of a food E621 monosodium glutamate 2017: group ADI of 30 mg/kg per day, expressed as glutamic acid. The panel noted that in some population groups intake from these additives exceeded both the ADI and doses associated with adverse effects in humans14
Sweetener Gives a sweet taste E951 aspartame, E955 sucralose, E950 acesulfame K, E960 steviol glycosides Aspartame 2013: ADI of 40 mg/kg per day (not applicable to people with phenylketonuria)15. Sucralose 2026: the ADI of 15 mg/kg per day kept16. Acesulfame K 2025: ADI raised from 9 to 15 mg/kg per day17. Steviol glycosides 2010: ADI of 4 mg/kg per day, expressed as steviol equivalents18

Reading the Table

"No numerical ADI needed" means EFSA, looking at the available data and the reported levels of use, saw no need to set an upper limit; it is not a recommendation to eat unlimited amounts. "Estimated to exceed the ADI" is a finding the panel flagged in its own exposure calculation. These notes mostly concern children and the highest consumers, which is why we kept them in the table. Other things to check on children's products are in our guide to baby and child snack labels.

How emulsifiers affect the gut microbiota is a separate line of research that sits outside the ADI calculation. We cover the studies on carboxymethylcellulose (E466), polysorbate 80 (E433) and carrageenan (E407) in our article on emulsifiers and the microbiome, along with the 2023 IARC classification of aspartame.

Special Warnings on the Label

Some additives require an extra statement outside the ingredients list:

  • Six colours (E102 tartrazine, E104 quinoline yellow, E110 sunset yellow, E122 carmoisine, E124 ponceau 4R, E129 allura red): the name or E number of the colour followed by "may have an adverse effect on activity and attention in children"1. The EU regulation requires the same statement (Annex V)21.
  • Aspartame: "contains aspartame (a source of phenylalanine)" or "contains a source of phenylalanine", depending on whether the ingredients list uses the E number or the name2, 22. The warning is for people with phenylketonuria; EFSA also states that the ADI does not apply to them15.
  • Polyols: foods with more than 10% added polyols carry "excessive consumption may produce laxative effects"2, 22.
  • Sweeteners: the statement "with sweetener(s)" (in Turkish "Tatlandırıcılı" or "Tatlandırıcı(lar) içerir") has been required in Turkey since the 2017 regulation. The amendment of 6 April 2024 requires it next to the name of the food wherever the name appears, set apart by font, style or background colour; businesses must comply by 31 December 20262, 20. In the EU, "with sweetener(s)" must accompany the name of the food22.

Different rules for baby and young-child foods

In infant formula, follow-on formula and complementary foods for infants and young children, only the additives that the regulation's annex lists specifically for these foods may be used1. The EU regulation has the same principle (Article 16)21. So do not assume that an additive found in an adult product can also be in baby food; read the label for the product group in front of you.

Emulsifiers and the Gut Microbiome: Behind the E Numbers

Carboxymethylcellulose (E466), polysorbate 80 (E433), carrageenan (E407) and the gut barrier.

← All articles

English · Türkçe

Bardino · 15 October 2026

Having an E number does not make an ingredient dangerous. Sodium bicarbonate (E500) and ascorbic acid (E300) have E numbers too. Emulsifiers, however, have become a topic of their own over the past ten years: mouse experiments published in 2015 and a small, double-blind human study published in 2022 suggest that some emulsifiers can affect the gut microbiota.

What Emulsifiers Are and Why They Are Everywhere

Emulsifiers are molecules that keep two phases that normally do not mix, such as oil and water, in a stable blend. In a home kitchen, egg yolk (lecithin) stabilises mayonnaise. In manufactured food you will see names such as mono- and diglycerides (E471), carboxymethylcellulose (E466), polysorbate 80 (E433), carrageenan (E407) and soya lecithin (E322). A study of the four largest UK supermarket chains found at least one emulsifier in 51.7% of the 12,844 products in food categories that supply ultra-processed food1.

The Turning Point: Chassaing and Gewirtz, 2015

In a study published in Nature in 2015, Benoit Chassaing and Andrew Gewirtz showed that in mice, relatively low concentrations of carboxymethylcellulose and polysorbate 80 were associated with bacteria moving into the mucus layer that normally protects the gut surface2. Normal mice developed low-grade inflammation and obesity/metabolic syndrome; mice prone to colitis developed marked colitis. The authors suggested that the broad use of emulsifiers might be contributing to the rise in obesity and chronic inflammatory diseases. It was, however, a study in mice.

Human Evidence: A 2022 Double-Blind Controlled-Feeding Study

The study that took the question to humans was published in Gastroenterology in 2022: of 16 healthy adults, 9 ate an emulsifier-free diet and 7 ate the same diet with 15 g a day of carboxymethylcellulose (E466) added, for 11 days3. In the E466 group the researchers saw:

  • A modest increase in abdominal discomfort after meals.
  • A change in the gut microbiota that reduced its diversity.
  • Reductions in the faecal metabolome, especially in short-chain fatty acids and free amino acids.
  • In 2 of the 7 people, bacteria moving into the inner mucus layer, which is normally kept free of bacteria.

What changes seen in 16 people over 11 days mean clinically in the long term is unclear. A 2021 review found that research on the health effects of emulsifiers is limited to laboratory (in vitro) and mouse studies and to small, short-term studies in humans4.

Observational Data: NutriNet-Santé, 2023-2024

Analyses of the French NutriNet-Santé cohort published in 2023 and 2024 linked higher intake of some emulsifiers with the following:

  • Cardiovascular disease (95,442 people): higher risk with celluloses (E460-E468, including E466) and with mono- and diglycerides of fatty acids (E471, E472)5.
  • Type 2 diabetes (104,139 people): higher risk with several emulsifiers, including carrageenans (HR 1.03 per extra 100 mg a day), guar gum E412 and xanthan gum E415 (HR 1.11 and 1.08 per extra 500 mg a day)6.
  • Cancer (92,000 people): comparing higher with lower intake, E471 was associated with overall cancer (HR 1.15), breast cancer (1.24) and prostate cancer (1.46), and total carrageenans with breast cancer (1.32). No association was found with colorectal cancer7.

These are observational data and do not prove cause and effect; the authors stress that the findings need to be replicated in other populations7.

Carrageenan: EFSA's temporary decision

In its 2018 re-evaluation, EFSA treated the group acceptable daily intake for carrageenan (E407), 75 mg per kilogram of body weight per day, as temporary because of uncertainties in the chemistry, exposure and toxicology data, and asked for the data to be improved within five years8. Take questions about baby and children's foods to your child's doctor.

Aspartame (E951): The IARC 2B Classification

In July 2023 the World Health Organization's cancer research agency, IARC, classified aspartame as Group 2B: possibly carcinogenic to humans9. On the same day, the Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives (JECFA) kept the acceptable daily intake (ADI) at 0-40 mg per kilogram of body weight per day9. EFSA's 2013 assessment had also found the same 40 mg/kg limit protective for the general population10. In practice:

  • According to WHO, safety is not a major concern at the doses commonly used; a 70 kg adult with no other source of aspartame would need to drink more than 9-14 cans of diet soft drink a day to exceed the limit, assuming 200-300 mg of aspartame per can9.
  • "Group 2B" describes the strength of the evidence, not the size of the risk at a given dose9. This group is "possibly" (2B), not "probably" (2A); caffeic acid is in Group 2B as well11.
  • Both bodies say more research is needed9.

EFSA's Re-evaluation Programme

Under Commission Regulation (EU) No 257/2010, EFSA is re-evaluating the food additives authorised before 20 January 2009 against current scientific evidence12. During and after this process:

  • In 2021 EFSA concluded that titanium dioxide (E171) could no longer be considered safe as a food additive; the EU then banned its use in food with Commission Regulation (EU) 2022/63 (the transition period ended on 7 August 2022)13.
  • The maximum permitted levels for nitrites and nitrates (E249-E252) were lowered by Commission Regulation (EU) 2023/2108; the new limits for meat products apply from 9 October 202514.
  • Foods containing certain colours (E102, E104, E110, E122, E124, E129) must carry the warning "may have an adverse effect on activity and attention in children". This rule does not come from the re-evaluation programme but from Annex V of Regulation (EC) No 1333/200815.

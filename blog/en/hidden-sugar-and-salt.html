Hidden Sugar and Salt on Turkish Supermarket Shelves

WHO limits, the different names sugar goes by on labels, and measured data on where salt in Turkey comes from.

← All articles

English · Türkçe

Bardino · 15 October 2026

In Turkey, people aged 15 and over eat an average of 9.9 g of salt a day: almost twice the 5 g recommended by the World Health Organization1. Much of that salt does not come from the salt shaker but from bread and processed foods7. Sugar works in a similar way: a product can contain sugar even when the word "sugar" does not appear on the label.

The definition of "free sugars" matters: besides sugars added to food, the sugars in honey, syrups, fruit juice and fruit juice concentrates are free sugars, while the sugars naturally present in fresh fruit and vegetables and in milk are not2. In a separate 2023 guideline, WHO advised against using non-sugar sweeteners to control weight or to reduce the risk of non-communicable diseases (a conditional recommendation that does not cover people with pre-existing diabetes); the systematic review behind it suggests that these sweeteners bring no long-term benefit in reducing body fat3.

The Different Names for Sugar on a Label

A product can be loaded with sugar even if you do not see the word "sugar" in the ingredients. The SugarScience programme of the University of California, San Francisco (UCSF) says there are at least 61 different names for sugar on food labels4. Some of the names on its list:

  • Names ending in "-ose": dextrose, fructose, glucose, maltose, sucrose.
  • Syrups: corn syrup, high-fructose corn syrup (HFCS), maple syrup, malt syrup, rice syrup, golden syrup, agave nectar.
  • Juices: fruit juice, fruit juice concentrate.
  • Natural-sounding names: honey, molasses, date sugar, cane sugar, palm sugar, coconut sugar.
  • Others: maltodextrin, dextrin.

On Turkish shelves you will also see pekmez, a traditional syrup usually made from grapes; WHO counts the sugars in syrups as free sugars2.

By law, the ingredients list is in descending order of weight5. So if a product uses two or three different kinds of sugar, each can appear separately further down the list, whereas if they were all written as one "sugar" it might be at the top. That is why it is worth adding up the sugar names one by one.

Do not rely on "sugar-free" alone

Under both EU and Turkish rules, a "sugar-free" claim means the product contains no more than 0.5 g of sugars per 100 g or 100 ml6. A "no added sugar" claim requires that no sugars, and no other food used for its sweetening properties, have been added6. If the product contains its own natural sugars, both sets of rules require the label to also say "contains naturally occurring sugars"6. Still, look at the ingredients list.

Hidden Salt: Bread and Breakfast Foods

SALTURK II combined 24-hour urine collection with a food record kept on the same day, in Ankara, Istanbul, Izmir and Konya. According to the food records, the largest source of salt was bread (34%), followed by salt added during cooking (30%), various processed foods (21%) and salt added at the table (11%). Of the salt from processed foods, 62% came from breakfast items such as cheese and olives, 16% from pickles, and 10% from meat, poultry, fish and meat products7. So putting the salt shaker away is not enough on its own.

Turkey's Ministry of Health launched a salt reduction programme in 2011; the recommended salt content of bread was lowered from 1.75 g to 1.5 g per 100 g in 20127. The measurements point to a fall: 18.0 g a day in the first SALTURK study, 14.8 g in SALTURK II in 20127, and 9.9 g in the 2017 STEPS survey1. But the methods differ (24-hour urine versus a single spot urine sample), so the figures cannot be compared directly, and it is not known how much of the fall is due to the programme. Even 9.9 g is almost twice the limit WHO recommends.

Converting sodium to salt

Labels often give "sodium". To find the salt (NaCl) equivalent: sodium × 2.5 = salt; 1 g of salt is equivalent to 400 mg of sodium9.

For Children, the Problem Starts Earlier

A 2019 report from the WHO Regional Office for Europe looked at baby and young-child foods in four countries, with data collected in 2017-2018 in Vienna, Sofia, Budapest and Haifa. In Austria, Bulgaria and Hungary, about half or more of the products got more than 30% of their energy from sugars; in Israel the figure was 18%. A sweetening ingredient, such as sugar or concentrated fruit juice, was found in about a third or more of the products10. What the report measured was total sugars (including natural sugars from fruit and milk), so it does not mean every product exceeded WHO's free sugars limit. What to check on children's products is covered in our guide to baby and child snack labels.

Comparing the Label with the WHO Limit

Turning grams on the label into a share of the daily limit is a simple calculation:

  • Salt: if a 25 g slice of bread contains 0.375 g of salt (1.5 g per 100 g), that is 7.5% of WHO's daily limit of 5 g.
  • Sugar: on a 2,000 kcal day, the 10% limit for free sugars is about 50 g. But the "sugars" line on the label also includes the natural sugar in milk (lactose) and fruit, which is not part of WHO's free sugars definition. So part of the sugar shown on a flavoured yoghurt is not free sugar.

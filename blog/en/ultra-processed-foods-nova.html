Ultra-Processed Foods: The NOVA Classification

What did the 2024 BMJ umbrella review of about 9.9 million participants find? And how do you spot NOVA 4 on the shelf?

← All articles

English · Türkçe

Bardino · 15 October 2026

"Processed food" is often used too broadly. Pasteurised milk is processed, and so are frozen peas. The useful distinction comes from the NOVA classification developed by Carlos Monteiro and colleagues at the University of São Paulo: what matters is not processing as such, but its degree and the nature of the industrial formulation that results1.

The Four NOVA Groups

  1. Group 1, unprocessed or minimally processed foods: grains, legumes, vegetables, fruits, nuts, milk, meat. Processes such as drying, pasteurisation, refrigeration and freezing do not add salt, sugar, oils or fats1.
  2. Group 2, processed culinary ingredients: oils and fats, sugar and salt. They are not eaten on their own but used with Group 1 foods to make dishes1.
  3. Group 3, processed foods: made by adding Group 2 substances to Group 1 foods, for example breads and cheeses; canning and bottling are typical ways of preserving them1.
  4. Group 4, ultra-processed foods (UPF): industrial formulations. Typical ingredients include hydrolysed proteins, soya protein isolate, high-fructose corn syrup, hydrogenated or interesterified oils and maltodextrin, together with "cosmetic" additives such as flavours, flavour enhancers, colours, emulsifiers, sweeteners and thickeners1.

How to spot NOVA 4 on the shelf

The practical approach Monteiro and colleagues propose: check whether the ingredients list contains at least one item characteristic of NOVA 4. These are either substances never or rarely used in kitchens (such as high-fructose corn syrup, hydrogenated or interesterified oils, hydrolysed proteins) or classes of additives used to make the product more palatable or attractive (such as flavours, flavour enhancers, colours, emulsifiers, sweeteners, thickeners)1.

The 2024 BMJ Umbrella Review: 45 Analyses, About 9.9 Million Participants

The umbrella review by Lane and colleagues, published in the BMJ in February 2024, brought together 45 pooled analyses from previously published meta-analyses (9,888,373 participants in total)2. In 71% of the analyses (32 of 45), greater exposure to ultra-processed food was associated with a worse health outcome. Selected results:

Under the grading the researchers set in advance, the evidence was "convincing" (class I) for cardiovascular death, type 2 diabetes (dose-response), anxiety and common mental disorders, and "highly suggestive" (class II) for the other results above. Of the remaining analyses, 21 were graded class III-IV and 13 class V. The quality of the evidence assessed with GRADE was mostly low, however: of the 45 analyses, 22 were low, 19 very low and only 4 moderate quality. These are observational studies; the authors write that the findings provide a rationale for developing and evaluating population-based and public health measures to reduce exposure to ultra-processed food2.

Just Correlation, or Cause?

Observational studies do not prove cause and effect. That is why the randomised controlled trial run by Kevin Hall and his team at the US National Institutes of Health (NIH) in 2019 matters3. Twenty adults admitted to a research unit ate an ultra-processed diet for two weeks and an unprocessed diet for two weeks. The meals offered were matched for calories, energy density, macronutrients, sugar, sodium and fibre, and participants could eat as much as they wanted. The result:

  • On the UPF diet, participants ate an average of 508 kcal more per day.
  • Over those two weeks they gained 0.9 kg on average; on the unprocessed diet they lost 0.9 kg.
  • The study was not designed to find the cause of this difference; the authors note that meals were eaten faster on the UPF diet.

The trial showed under controlled conditions that an ultra-processed diet can lead to higher calorie intake even when the meals offered are matched for nutrients. But it involved 20 people over four weeks; on its own it is not enough for conclusions about the long term.

UPF Consumption in Turkey

The data we have for Turkey come from studies with small samples. For example, among 50 healthy children aged 5 to 17 who attended a social paediatrics outpatient clinic for check-ups, 24.5% of daily energy came from ultra-processed foods4. This result cannot be generalised to the whole country. For comparison, among adults in the United States the share rose from 53.5% in 2001-2002 to 57% in 2017-20185.

"Organic" does not take a product out of NOVA 4

NOVA classifies a product by its ingredients and additive classes1, not by how its raw materials were farmed. A product can be certified organic and still contain, for example, a protein isolate, which places it in NOVA 4.

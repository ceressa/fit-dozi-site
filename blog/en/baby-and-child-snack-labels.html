Baby and Child Snacks: What to Check on the Label

Sugar, salt, sweeteners and age-specific warnings: which line of the label matters, and at what age.

← All articles

English · Türkçe

Bardino · 15 October 2026

On a child's snack, check four things on the label: free sugars (including fruit juice concentrate), salt, sweeteners and age-specific warnings. The World Health Organization advises that children aged 6-23 months should not be given foods high in sugar, salt or trans fats, sugar-sweetened drinks or non-sugar sweeteners1. For older children the limits are looser but do not disappear. Phrases on the front such as "for your baby", "with fruit" or "no added sugar" do not answer these four questions; the answers are on the back.

Limits by Age

WHO's 2023 guideline on complementary feeding, in its Recommendation 5 on unhealthy foods and drinks, sets out four points for children aged 6-23 months: foods high in sugar, salt and trans fats should not be consumed (strong recommendation, low-certainty evidence); sugar-sweetened beverages should not be consumed (strong recommendation, low-certainty evidence); non-sugar sweeteners should not be consumed (strong recommendation, very low-certainty evidence); and 100% fruit juice should be limited (conditional recommendation, low-certainty evidence)1. WHO also advises that salt and sugars should not be added to complementary foods2.

The upper limits by age from the NHS, the UK's National Health Service, give a practical yardstick when you read a label:

The NHS page on foods to avoid giving babies and young children goes further: do not give children aged 4 and under food or drinks that contain sugar or sweeteners; this includes the natural sugars in honey, syrups, fruit juice and smoothies, while whole fruit itself is fine5.

Sugar: What Does "No Added Sugar" Mean?

Under WHO's definition, free sugars are mono- and disaccharides added by the manufacturer, cook or consumer, plus the sugars naturally present in honey, syrups, fruit juices and fruit juice concentrates2. So the sugar in a baby biscuit sweetened with apple juice concentrate is free sugar, even though it "comes from fruit".

In the Turkish Food Codex the condition for "no added sugar" (şeker ilavesiz or ilave şeker içermez) is strict: the product must not contain any added mono- or disaccharides or any other food used for its sweetening properties. If the product contains sugar naturally, the label must also say "contains naturally occurring sugars" (DOĞAL OLARAK ŞEKER İÇERİR)6. The EU sets the same condition13. On products without this claim, look at the ingredients list: fruit juice concentrate, fruit purée, honey, pekmez (fruit syrup), glucose syrup and dextrose may each appear separately.

The "sugars" line in the nutrition table does not separate added sugar from the lactose in milk or the sugar in fruit; the regulation defines it as "all monosaccharides and disaccharides, excluding polyols"7. Even a plain yoghurt whose ingredients are only milk and cultures has a value on this line; it comes from the milk's own sugar. The way to spot added sugar is the ingredients list.

Salt: Convert Sodium to Salt, Treat the Pack as a Portion

If the label gives sodium, multiply by 2.5 to get the salt equivalent; that is how the regulation defines salt7, 11. Because values are given per 100 g, the next step is to convert to the pack. Example: a 30 g pack of crackers with 1.2 g of salt per 100 g contains 0.36 g of salt, about a fifth of the NHS daily limit of 2 g for ages 1-3. Salt from cheese, bread and olives eaten the same day counts towards that limit too. For where salt comes from in the Turkish diet, see our article on hidden sugar and salt.

Sweeteners: More Visible on Labels Now

In Turkey, foods containing sweeteners have had to say "with sweetener(s)" (Tatlandırıcılı or Tatlandırıcı(lar) içerir) next to the name of the food since the 2017 regulation; if sugar is also added, "with sugar(s) and sweetener(s)"7. The amendment of 6 April 2024 requires this statement, in emphasised type, wherever the name of the food appears; businesses must comply by 31 December 20268. In the EU, "with sweetener(s)" must accompany the name of the food11. It still helps to look for the class name "sweetener" in the ingredients list. Products with aspartame also carry a "source of phenylalanine" warning, and products with more than 10% added polyols carry "excessive consumption may produce laxative effects"7, 11.

In infant formula, follow-on formula and complementary foods for infants and young children, only the additives that the regulation's annex specifically permits for these foods may be used9; the EU has the same principle12. This rule applies only to products that fall under the legal definition of foods for infants and young children; a general snack given to a child is not covered by the same restriction. EFSA's assessments of individual additives are in our E numbers list.

Age-Specific Warnings

  • No honey under 1. The NHS says honey can occasionally contain bacteria that produce toxins in a baby's bowel, which can lead to infant botulism5. If you are choosing a product for a baby under 1, look for honey in the ingredients list too.
  • No whole nuts under 5. Because of the risk of choking, the NHS advises not giving whole nuts to children under 5; crushed or ground nuts can be given from the start of weaning5. For how to introduce new foods with allergy in mind, see our guide to food allergy labels for children.
  • Caffeine warning. Drinks with more than 150 mg of caffeine per litre, other than coffee- or tea-based drinks whose name includes coffee or tea, carry "High caffeine content. Not recommended for children or pregnant or breast-feeding women"; other foods with caffeine added for a physiological purpose carry a warning that they are not recommended for children7. The EU rule has the same wording11.
  • Six colours. Foods containing E102, E104, E110, E122, E124 or E129 must carry "may have an adverse effect on activity and attention in children"9, 12. Look for this line on brightly coloured sweets and drinks.

Products labelled "from 4 months"

The WHO Regional Office for Europe notes that many commercial products are marketed as suitable from 4 months of age, and that sweet purées and snacks high in free sugars dominate the shelves; WHO recommends exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months10. When to start complementary foods should be decided by your child's doctor, not by the age on the pack.

A One-Minute Check at the Shelf

  1. Does the front say "with sweetener(s)" or "flavoured"?
  2. Are sugar, fruit juice concentrate, syrup or honey among the first three ingredients?
  3. Multiply the sugar and salt per 100 g by the pack weight and compare with the age limits in the table.
  4. Check the age-specific warnings (honey, whole nuts, caffeine, colours).
  5. Read the allergens in emphasis and any "may contain" statement.

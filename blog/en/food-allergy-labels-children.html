Food Allergy in Children: A Guide to Reading Labels

The 14 mandatory allergens, cross-contamination statements and current guidelines.

← All articles

English · Türkçe

Bardino · 15 October 2026

According to the GA²LEN 2022 guideline, food allergy affects about 2-4% of children and adults3. A 2023 meta-analysis pooling European studies found self-reported food allergy to be far more common (point prevalence 13.1%), but the rate confirmed by a food challenge test was 0.8%1. In Turkey, records of 949 children (mean age 28 months) followed at the well-child clinics of two university hospitals showed a food allergy rate of 4.3%2. For a child with an allergy, reading the label in the shop is the most basic precaution.

The 14 Mandatory Allergen Groups

Turkey's Turkish Food Codex Regulation on Food Labelling and Provision of Food Information to Consumers (26.01.2017) states that it was prepared with the EU's Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 in mind, as part of aligning with EU law4. It requires the 14 allergen groups in its Annex 1 to be emphasised in the ingredients list of prepacked foods so that they stand out from the rest of the list, by font, style or background colour4. The EU regulation lists the same 14 groups (Annex II) and has the same emphasis rule (Article 21)8:

  1. Cereals containing gluten (wheat, rye, barley, oats, kamut, spelt and their hybrids)
  2. Crustaceans (shrimp, lobster and so on)
  3. Eggs
  4. Fish
  5. Peanuts
  6. Soybeans
  7. Milk (including lactose)
  8. Tree nuts (almonds, hazelnuts, walnuts, cashews, pecans, Brazil nuts, pistachios, macadamias)
  9. Celery
  10. Mustard
  11. Sesame
  12. Sulphur dioxide and sulphites (more than 10 mg/kg or 10 mg/litre, expressed as total SO₂)
  13. Lupin
  14. Molluscs (mussels, octopus and so on)

How to Read "May Contain"

Statements such as "may contain hazelnuts" or "milk is handled in the same factory" are voluntary: they report a risk of cross-contamination on the production line. The EU regulation leaves the rules on information about the possible unintentional presence of allergens to implementing acts to be adopted by the Commission (Article 36(3)(a)); the regulation itself sets no threshold8. The Turkish regulation sets no threshold for these statements either; the Ministry's guideline allows them only after a risk assessment and gives no number4, 10. So the amount of contamination behind the same words can differ from one producer to another.

The GA²LEN 2022 guideline on managing food allergy also lists the lack of a regulated framework for precautionary allergen labelling as a barrier, which makes these statements hard to interpret3. Talk to your child's allergy specialist about whether products with "may contain" statements are suitable for your child.

A well-known reference on thresholds is the VITAL programme of the Allergen Bureau in Australia and New Zealand. VITAL 3.0, published in 2019, used as its reference the doses expected to trigger objective symptoms in the most sensitive 1% of allergic people; for peanut and milk, this dose is 0.2 mg of protein. VITAL 4.0 was released in 2024; the 0.2 mg figure is the VITAL 3.0 value5.

Read the label again every time

If your child has eaten a product before without problems, do not assume the next batch is safe too. Manufacturers can change recipes, and ingredient suppliers can change. Read the label again every time you shop.

When Your Child Tries a New Food

A 2019 report from the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) says there is no evidence that delaying allergenic foods such as peanut, egg and fish beyond 4-6 months prevents allergic disease, and that introducing peanut early may prevent peanut allergy7. The 2020 guideline of the European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology (EAACI) recommends introducing well-cooked egg, and in populations where peanut allergy is common, peanut in an age-appropriate form, as part of complementary feeding; according to the studies, the most effective age is 4-6 months6.

This approach builds on the LEAP study of 2015: among 640 infants with severe eczema, egg allergy or both, in the group whose skin-prick test was negative at the start, peanut allergy at age 5 was 13.7% in those who avoided peanut and 1.9% in those who ate it regularly9. In practice:

  • If your baby has severe eczema or a known food allergy, talk to your paediatrician or allergy specialist before introducing new allergens.
  • Ask your paediatrician when to start and in what form.
  • If there is a reaction, keep the product; it helps the doctor make a diagnosis.

At restaurants and when eating out

Ask for allergen information in writing. Under the Turkish labelling regulation, allergen information must also be given to the consumer for foods offered without packaging; it can be provided on a menu, a board or a leaflet. The general compliance period that the regulation gave to businesses already operating ended on 31 December 20194. In the EU, allergen information for non-prepacked food is mandatory as well, and member states decide how it is provided (Article 44)8.

Allergens in Fit Dozi

In Fit Dozi you can set a separate allergen list for each family member.
